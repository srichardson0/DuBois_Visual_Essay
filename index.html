Recreating W.E.B. Du Bois's Data Visualizations

The Great Migration: 1900 - 2020

By Sasha Richardson

In 1900, W.E.B. Du Bois created groundbreaking data visualizations for the Paris Exposition to counter racist narratives and reveal the reality of Black life in America. His "Distribution of Negroes" map used precise colors and elegant design to show geographic concentration in the South.

This visualization honors Du Bois's innovative approach by applying his exact visual language—his color palette, typography, and layout—to tell the story of 120 years of demographic transformation. Using Census data, I trace how the Great Migration reshaped the American landscape and transformed political representation.

1900: The Starting Point

When Du Bois created his original map, nearly 90% of African Americans lived in the South, concentrated in the "Black Belt" stretching from Virginia to Texas. The legacy of slavery created this geographic pattern; high density in former plantation regions.

1960: The Great Migration

Between 1900 and 1960, over 6 million African Americans moved from the rural South to industrial cities in the North and West—the largest internal migration in American history. Chicago, Detroit, New York, Philadelphia, and Los Angeles became major centers of Black life.

Despite this massive demographic shift and the growth of urban Black communities, political representation remained minimal. The civil rights movement was fighting for voting rights that would finally begin to translate population into power.

Annotations: Arrows indicate the percent growth from 1900 to 1960. ★ Stars indicate states with African American representatives.

2020: The Modern Era

By 2020, the map tells a more complex story. The "Reverse Migration" brought many African Americans back to the South, drawn by economic opportunity, lower costs, and cultural connections. Meanwhile, the West Coast and major metros across all regions show sustained density.

Political representation has transformed dramatically: 58 African Americans serve in Congress , with representation extending from coast to coast. Yet this progress still falls short of proportional representation—13.6% of the population holds 11% of House seats.

What changed: The density patterns reflect both continued urbanization and the attractiveness of Southern metros like Atlanta, Houston, and Charlotte as economic engines.

Then & Now: Du Bois's Vision Meets Modern Data

Compare Du Bois's original 1900 map with our 2020 recreation. While his map documented the constraints of post-slavery geography, today's map reveals the outcome of migration, struggle, and hard-won political gains.

Du Bois understood that data visualization was a tool for truth-telling and justice. By applying his aesthetic approach to contemporary data, we see both how far we've come and how his fundamental insight remains relevant: representation matters, and making inequality visible is the first step toward changing it.

About This Project

This interactive visualization recreates W.E.B. Du Bois's innovative design approach using modern data on African American population distribution and political representation. By maintaining fidelity to Du Bois's original color palette and typographic choices, it demonstrates how his century-old visual language remains powerful for contemporary data storytelling.
